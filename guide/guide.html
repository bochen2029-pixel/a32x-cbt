    1What it is

    A spaceplane takes off from KDFW runway 17C like an aeroplane, keeps climbing, reaches a 500 km orbit and closes on a ring station there. It docks at the station's hub. Its crew walks inside, where the floor curves up ahead and behind and meets itself overhead. It lands on the station's open flight deck as a jump jet lands on a ship, and takes off again. Then it flies home to 17C, through entry, and stops on the centreline.

    It is called Runway to Ring, and in the repository the orbit sketch. It was made on 12 November 2026, on the owner's word and outside the roadmap of the repository it lives in, a32x-cbt, whose product is an A320 trainer, and taken further on 21 November. It stands apart from the A320: its own small flight core, its own page, and no file of the Airbus product changed.

    The one fiction

    The drive gives 706 kN, 1.2 times the take-off weight, at 2,000 s of specific impulse. That is the only thing in the sketch that physics does not allow today. At 450 s, the best of chemical rockets, half the craft's mass in propellant would buy about 3 km/s, a third of what orbit takes. At 2,000 s the crew reaches orbit having spent 10.92 km/s of rocket-equation velocity and keeps 4.39 t of propellant, about 2.7 km/s more. Everything else is ordinary mechanics: gravity, the 1976 standard atmosphere, the lift of a slender delta, a spinning wheel.

    The loop, flown by the crew

    The page lets you take the controls at any point and give them back: the crew picks up from wherever the craft is. The walk inside the Ring and the landing on the flight deck are side trips from the hub; the tour flies them all. The Sun, the Moon and the stars stand where they really were over KDFW at 19:00 UTC on 12 November 2026, one in the afternoon in Texas, or on any date from 1900 to 2100.

    How the stick flies

    In the air the stick moves pitch and bank targets that the flight law holds, turning in balance, much as a fly-by-wire airliner's law does: let go and the craft keeps the attitude it was given. Above the air, where the dynamic pressure is under 400 Pa (the law hands back below 200 Pa), the stick commands rates and the craft holds the attitude it is left at, or points where 1 to 4 say. The status line says which law is flying: AIR LAW, or RATE and the hold.

    On a touch screen a stick sits under the left thumb and a throttle under the right; between them the six buttons used most (TOUR, CREW, TO ORBIT, VIEW, TIME +, MORE), and MORE opens the rest, BRAKES among them (figure ). A gamepad with the standard mapping works as the table says, and its buttons fire on the press.

    3The loop, part by part

    Each part below says what it does, how to fly it, and the numbers its tests hold it to. The core is in parts too (section 4), and each part's test program is named in the tables.

    3.1The take-off and the climb to orbit

    The crew holds the brakes for 20 s, then flies the whole ascent: 12½ minutes to orbit. It takes off due south, as 17C points, rotates at 95 m/s, and from 300 m turns left, banked 30°, to the launch azimuth, 090. A pitch schedule carries it through the air, the throttle eased through the region of highest dynamic pressure. Above the air it steers by a cut-down version of the Space Shuttle's powered explicit guidance: the sine of the thrust's elevation is linear in time, and the line is solved again each step so that the burn ends at the Ring's height with no climb left and a round orbit's speed.

    What it flies on

    • The Earth: a sphere of 6,371 km, its gravity a point mass's, turning once a sidereal day. The ground is level to the local plumb line, gravity and the Earth's turn together, so a parked craft stays parked (it moves under 1 cm in 30 s, ground_settles).
    • The air: the 1976 standard atmosphere to 86 km, held within 0.5 % of its tables to 80 km; above it, exponentials through the standard's densities at 86, 120, 200 and 500 km, within 20 %. Still, unless a wind is set (section 3.7).
    • The craft: a delta lifting body of 150 m² and 18 m span, its lift Polhamus's for a slender delta blending to Newtonian impact from Mach 1.5 to 5, with static and damping derivatives and three gear legs. A rate loop by dynamic inversion flies it inside what the surfaces (by dynamic pressure), the small thrusters and the drive's gimbal can give.
    • The step: 0.02 s, fifty a second, by fourth-order Runge–Kutta in an inertial frame. Time can run 100 times faster; past a cap the page drops time rather than stretch a step.

    Flying it yourself

    Take the controls on the runway or anywhere in the climb. On the runway Q E steer the nose wheel; full throttle (Z) and the brakes off (B; the status line says so while they hold the craft) start the roll, and a pull at about 95 m/s lifts the nose. In the air the stick sets pitch and bank and the law holds them. A take-off by hand at full power is airborne within 30 s; half the stick to the right asks 22.5° of bank, and let go, the law holds 18 to 27° with under 1° of sideslip (hand_flown_takeoff). Give the controls back at any height and the crew takes the climb from where it is: from a hand-flown climb at 3 km it still reaches orbit, 500.6 × 499.8 km at 727 s (crew_takes_over). While you fly, the flight director (section 3.8) shows what the crew's guidance would do, as cues to fly by.

    Above the air there is nothing to bank against: the stick turns the craft at a rate, and 1 holds it where it is left, 2 and 3 point it along or against its motion through space, 4 at the Ring. Burning prograde raises the far side of the orbit; the map (section 3.10) shows the orbit's new shape as it changes.

    Why 32.84°? KDFW is at 32.9° north. A launch due east from there gives an orbit inclined at about the site's latitude, the least inclination reachable without a turn in space; the orbit's plane then passes over KDFW once a day, which is what the flight home waits for.

    3.2Orbit and the Ring

    The Ring waits in a round 500 km orbit in the crew's plane, 25 km ahead of where the crew arrives: at reset the core flies the crew once, unseen, to find that place. A real launch window is the inverse problem, the launch timed to the station; this sketch does not solve it. The Ring's spin axis is fixed among the stars, as a spinning wheel's is, set along the track where the crew arrives, so the hub's port faces the arriving craft.

    3.3The approach and docking

    The craft's port is a probe on its nose, on the docking axis, its head 18 m ahead of the centre of mass and 2 m past the nose: the pilot looks straight down the axis from the cockpit, and the laws that point the nose point the port. The hub's port is a drogue, a cone with a socket, at the centre of the hub's face on the Ring's axis, painted round with a target 36 m across: a bullseye, an alignment cross, and the key, a broad orange bar. The face turns with the Ring, 2.54° a second, once in 142 s. To dock is to line up on the axis, roll into phase with the key and turn with it, right wing down at the Ring's spin, and close slowly.

A contact that uses the probe's whole 0.4 m stroke, about 3 m/s, is a crash. Caught, the craft is drawn in over 6 s. Docked, it is part of the hub: its place, velocity, attitude and rates are worked out from the Ring's at every step, never integrated, so nothing drifts. Letting go (P) pushes it off at 0.15 m/s along the axis, still turning with the port; 1 stops the turn.

    Every other part of the craft is a few spheres, swept along each step against the Ring: the band, the rim walls and their lips, the six spokes, the hub with its collar, the radiators, and the solar mast and its arrays, which do not turn with the Ring, so the sweep meets them as they stood at each moment of the step. A touch is a crash, and the status line says what hit what: the right wing on the roof, the tail on a spoke, the probe on a radiator. Between the spokes, and out through the rim's opening, the way is free.

    3.4The walk inside the Ring

    Off the craft, through the hatch into the hub, down a spoke in a lift, and out onto the floor. Enter or WALK starts it when the craft is docked at the hub; at the hatch, E goes back aboard and the flight returns. The walk has its own core, core/walk.cpp, on the flight's fixed steps, and its own page, web/src/walk-view.js, which draws the whole frame while walking. The flight goes on underneath: the Ring keeps turning and orbiting.

    The physics is the Ring's turning frame and nothing else. A body in it feels the centrifugal pull ω²r outward and Coriolis's −2ω × v, and whatever holds it. Seen from outside, a body let go goes straight; the tests work each motion out again that way and hold the two equal. Up is toward the axis everywhere, so a jumper's turn about the axis speeds up on the way up, and a jump lands spinward, where on the Earth it lands to the west, against the spin.

    The lift's ride is chosen for its riders. Coriolis's push, 2ωv, is sideways and the same at any height, so the cruise is the speed that holds it to 1.5 m/s², 16.9 m/s. At the hub the riders weigh three hundredths of a g, and a car that left faster than they fall would leave them on its ceiling: it leaves, and arrives, at half their weight. At the floor it starts and stops at 1 m/s². While it moves the riders hold its rail.

    The page draws three passes: the real sky first (the flight's own Earth, air and stars, from where the eye is as the Ring turns, and only where the sky can be seen past the rim walls), then the Ring's inside over it, far and near. The Sun lights the band only where it gets in over a rim wall's top, worked out for each pixel. On 12 November it stands 23.7° out of the Ring's plane, so the middle of the band has about 50 s of sunlight in each turn of 142 s, and the strip under the wall on the Sun's side none. The roof's lamps and the Earth's light fill the rest. The walk follows the graphics tier: at low it leaves out the lattice's shadow, the Earth's light and the small things past 2.5 km.

    Two of the canon's numbers, measured. The larger game's canon (section 6) says a 2 m drop lands 3.8 cm off the plumb point and running 5 m/s spinward adds 4.6 % weight.     What is not in the walk

    People and furnishing; doors into the blocks, which are solid; water to swim in, since the parapets keep the walker out; the flight deck itself; the shadows of the blocks, the spokes and the hub on the floor; the way from the port to the hatch, which the walk skips; the Earth's tide across the Ring, six parts in ten thousand of a g. The town the walk is laid out on is its own, and the Ring drawn from outside paints another: the two do not match.

    What to try

    • Drop a ball (F) and look where it lands: a marker stands on the floor straight below where it was let go, and the ball comes down 37.7 mm antispinward of it. Throw one (G) spinward, then against the spin, and compare.
    • Jump in the lobby: 21 seconds in the air. Ride the lift down (E in a car) and watch the weight grow from 0.03 g to one g while the push sideways stays at 1.5 m/s²; run time faster (.) for the 5½ minutes it takes.

    3.5The flight deck

    The Ring has a deck: an open sector of its band, 1,500 m of the floor's arc and the band's whole 1 km across, midway between spokes 0 and 1. The roof stops at the sector's ends; at each end a pressure bulkhead 20 m thick stands from the floor to the roof. Three pads, 25 m in radius, lie on the centreline 400 m apart. It was the owner's idea: come in turning with the wheel, down a deck that curves like an airliner's glide path, then hover onto a pad as an F-35B does. The first crew (12 November) flew it so, a glide along the deck at 400 m and a hover onto the pad, for 1,794 kg of propellant from the hold point. Since 21 November the crew flies a plan of least propellant instead, and is at rest on the pad for 711.

    Why it is a hover

    In the Ring's own frame, which turns once in 142 s, the floor stands still and everything over it is pulled outward at ω²r, one g at the floor: a craft holding still over a spot of the deck must thrust toward the axis at that. Seen from the stars it is moving with the floor at ωr, 221.5 m/s, and the thrust bends its path round. A craft at rest among the stars feels no pull at all, and the deck sweeps past under it. Moving at u against the deck, along it, the pull is (ωr + u)²/r: flying spinward makes the craft heavier, antispinward lighter. So to land is to take up the deck's speed while coming down onto it, and the weight grows from nothing to one g as the speed against the deck goes (figure ).

    The craft as a jet

    The drive swivels, as an F-35B's does, from aft to straight down through the centre of mass, at 15° a second: the same drive, the same flow of propellant. Jet-borne, roll posts in the wings and a lift jet in the nose bleed up to 5 % of its thrust for attitude. Each law asks for the accelerations it wants along the deck, above it and across it, and gets the thrust from the exact equations of motion in a cylinder about the Ring's axis: the turning frame's pulls are worked out, not left to feedback, and the tides are fed forward.

    The crew's landing

    C, or LAND DECK, in orbit within 50 km of the Ring. The crew flies to the approach's hold point if the craft is not near it, round the Ring as docking goes; let go from the hub's port, it backs off to the hold point first, since a plan knows nothing of the hub, and from beside it the first burn carried the right wing into the hub (the tour's first run found it). From the hold point, a plan. In the Ring's own frame, which turns and whose centre falls freely, the motion is linear, the centrifugal and Coriolis pulls exact and the Earth's tide linear in the craft's offset; so the landing of least propellant is a convex problem once the thrust per kilogram is slackened by σ ≥ ‖u‖ and the mass taken by its logarithm (Açıkmeşe and Ploen's lossless convexification). It is a second-order cone program, posed by deck_plan.cpp in 40 intervals and solved in the core by an interior-point solver written for it, socp.cpp.

    A plan keeps inside a cone over the pad whose edge passes 40 m over the bulkheads' and rim walls' tops, and to its side of the Ring's plane among the spokes; its thrust is not toward the deck in its last 20 s and within 10° of up in its last 3, the drive lit at a quarter of its thrust or more through its last 20 s, and its force changes no faster than 5 m/s³, the swivel's 15° a second at full thrust. It asks for 85 % of the drive at most, the rest the tracking's, and ends at a gate 6 m over the pad's centre, sinking at 1.5 m/s. Its length is chosen by golden section, 13 plans one a step, for the least delta-v and 0.5 m/s for each second. The crew flies it: the plan's force fed forward 0.4 s ahead, for the throttle's lag, and a loop on the place and velocity against the plan's; the plan made again from where the craft is every 8 s while it coasts, every 3 s with a burn 12 s ahead, and in a burn when the craft is 10 m or 2 m/s off it. A plan made again that would take the craft under its gate's height before the gate is refused. The crew learns its drive in flight, from the acceleration it sees against the one its model expects.

    What that looks like from the hold point: a short burn out, the nose spinward; a weightless coast of a minute and a quarter while the pad comes round; and one burn, the airliner's approach become the F-35's slam, that takes up the deck's 192 m/s against it on the way down through the roof's gap, the swivel turning from aft to straight down as the weight comes on (figure ). From the gate the descent eases to 0.3 m/s 0.75 m over the legs' touch, and goes on under 5 m only over the pad's centre, still. On the deck the drive idles, the brakes go on, and the pilot has the craft, held on the deck, at one to one. The craft comes in tail first: the deck overtakes it, and the nose points the way it must be pushed. A landing is refused docked, crashed, out of orbit away from the Ring, far, fast past it, on the deck already, or under 1,000 kg of propellant; riding with the deck over it, the crew hovers to the nearest pad, or, faster than the hover's 6 m/s, plans the rest at once. A plan is made in a step of its own, one at most: 63 through a landing and a take-off, 17 ms each on average in WebAssembly under node, 34 ms at the most, so on a slow machine a plan's step can cost the page a frame.

    The crew's take-off

    7, or TAKE OFF, on the deck or hovering low over it: straight up 30 m off the pad, the nose turned antispinward; then a plan that is the landing's turned round, out through the roof's gap shedding the deck's speed and leaving it, a coast in, and a gentle stop, at most four tenths of the drive in its last 30 s, at the approach's hold point, where the approach's crew holds the craft. R flies home from there. On the deck, or riding with it, the core refuses RETURN, and the crew to orbit too: taken on a pad, the crew to orbit let go of the deck and struck a rim wall. The gear comes up only with no weight on it, on the deck as on the runway; the tour found the page's hands holding it up from the docked craft, and the gear folded on the pad.

    The gear meets the deck as it meets the runway, against the deck's motion, and its tyres stick to the spot where they come to rest, so a craft parked on a pad stays put under the Earth's tide on the Ring.     Why the deck is a vacuum. The Ring's air is held by its roof and sealed walls, and the deck sits behind bulkheads, open to space. An open Ring that held its air by spin alone, as in the film Elysium, would need to be far bigger: at 5 km the spin's pull is too weak over so short a height and the air would pour out of the open sides.     3.7The wind

    The air moves over the turning Earth only when a wind is set: oc_wind(seed, strength) in the core (core/wind.cpp), kept through everything but a reset.     • The mean wind. Up to 1,000 ft, the specification's log law, veering with height through 25° as an Ekman layer does.     3.9The Sun, the Moon and the stars

    The flight has a date: 12 November 2026 at 19:00:00 UTC unless the address says otherwise. That is one in the afternoon at KDFW, the Sun 38° up in the south-south-west and still 32° up where the crew reaches the Ring; a thin Moon, 11 % lit, stands 23° up in the south-south-east. From there the sky moves with the flight's clock: the Moon its half a degree an hour among the stars, the Sun its degree a day. Nothing in the flight depends on the date.

    The core works the sky out by J. Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms: the Sun to 0.01°, the Moon from the whole of the book's tables to about 10″, the Earth's sidereal time, precession and nutation, and the Moon's attitude by Cassini's laws.     4How it is built

    Two halves meet at one seam. The core is C++ and knows nothing of drawing; the page is JavaScript and owns nothing that moves. The page steps the core, reads its readouts, and draws them.

    The core

    flight/orbit/core is C++17 that includes only <cmath>, <cstdint> and <cstring>: no engine, no clock, no random source, no thread, no file. It advances only in whole fixed steps of 0.02 s, fifty a second, by fourth-order Runge–Kutta in an Earth-centred inertial frame; the readouts are given in the site's east, north and up, turning with the Earth. Time is the step count. The same inputs give the same state, bit for bit, and a hash of the whole state (oc_hash) shows it. The crew, the approach, docking, the deck's landing and take-off and the flight home live inside it as modes that command only rates, throttle, thrusters, the swivel, gear and brakes, as a pilot does.

    The same flight, every time. flight/orbit/build/orbit_run 10 760 flies the crew to 760 s and prints the flight every 10 s and a hash of the state. At 2638c37 the hash is 7b2cae00 natively (g++ 13.3 with glibc 2.39) and 03b21c18 as WebAssembly: each binary repeats itself bit for bit, and the two builds agree to every digit printed but not to the bit, since their C libraries' sin and exp differ in the last place. orbit_fingerprint prints the hash and every readout block's digest at 3,756 marks along twelve flights: built at two commits and diffed, it shows whether a change moved anything.

    It is one program in parts, each with its own contract: a public header with its constants, its C functions and a readouts block (an array of doubles the page reads after every step, named by an enum); its exports, one function a line, in core/exports/<part>.txt; its own state inside the core's State, so a run replays; and its own test program, test/test_<part>.cpp, whose tests CMake registers from lines reading // TEST: <name>. What the parts share is core/internal.h. The Ring's shape, its hub's fittings and the deck among them, is one set of numbers in orbit_core.h that the core, the drawing and the tests all use.

    The page

    flight/orbit/web is plain JavaScript modules and three.js 0.186, bundled with esbuild (both from flight/web's packages) into one HTML file that opens from file:// and fetches nothing: about 2.5 MB, of which the core as WebAssembly is 0.5 MB and the same core compiled to plain JavaScript another 0.6 MB. Where a host will not run WebAssembly the page runs the plain JavaScript and says so in its status line. Every texture is painted by code; no image, model, font or sound file ships. The coastlines, the towns, the Moon's features and the stars are data files made by tools in web/tools/ from public-domain or CC0 sources, each tool saying where its inputs come from.

    main.js keeps the core stepping at its fixed rate: each frame it adds the frame's time, times the warp, to a debt, runs whole steps, and drops the debt past a cap rather than stretch a step. The camera sits at the origin of what is drawn and everything is placed from it, in two passes: the far one (the Earth, its air, the stars, anything past 300 km) and the near one, so a centimetre at the wheels and the Earth's whole disc both have depth. The Earth is drawn per pixel, each ray meeting the sphere exactly, so it is as round from the runway as from orbit, under a single-scattering atmosphere. How big a picture to draw is adapt.js's, from the frames' times.

    Each part beyond the flight is a module of its own listed in features.js, with the same few hooks: init(ctx) once; command(name, ctx) for a key or a button, true if it took it; update(ctx) each frame before drawing; hud(ctx) for more readout lines; status(ctx) for the status line; and owns(ctx) with frame(ctx, dt) for a part that draws the whole frame itself, as the walk does. A part reads the core's readouts and gives commands; none writes the flight's state. The page keeps a handle, window.orbitSketch, for tests and for tools such as this guide's: the core, the page's state, and command(name).

    5Where it left off

    Nothing is furnished and nothing is finished: the README says so in its first paragraph. This is where each part stood on 21 November 2026.

    What is solid

    • The loop flies itself end to end. Take-off to orbit, the approach, docking, the walk, the deck's landing and take-off and the flight home each run under the crew from where the last one left the craft, and the whole loop runs in one test (lands_after_undocking). The tour flies all of it on the page, the deck included.
    • The core is deterministic and watched. One hash of the whole state, replay in pieces through every crew, conservation and work-energy ledgers with derived tolerances, no NaN under abuse, every readout re-derived, every constant of the flight certified.
    • The physics is stated, not tuned. The walk's Coriolis and the deck's hover are worked out against the view from outside the Ring; the deck's landing is a convex problem solved to its stated tolerances, set against an oracle; the Moon is held to Horizons; the flight home survives a wrong model 200 times in 200 within the narrower pool.
    • The page runs anywhere a browser does, from a phone to a desktop card, from one file, with no network, and holds its frame rate; it has been played by hand, by keys, clicks and taps, and its tests play it so in Chromium.

    The sketch is a proof that the airframe, the drive and the Ring work together, flown in a browser. The game it points at, with the working title Meridian Ring, is meant for Unreal Engine 5.8. Nothing of the Unreal version exists yet. What exists is a kit to start it from cold.

    The licence, decided: MIT

    The sketch was GPLv3, because everything in flight/ is, by this repository's rule. Unreal Engine's EULA forbids combining the engine with GPL code, and the GPL wants the whole of a combined work under the GPL, which Epic's code cannot be. So the core, as GPLv3, could not go into a shipped Unreal game. But it was the owner's to relicense: it links no third-party GPL code (only <cmath>, <cstdint>, <cstring>), takes nothing from A32NX or JSBSim, and what it took from outside is method, public domain or CC0.

    On 2026-11-21 the owner did: MIT, "and be done with it". flight/orbit/LICENSE carries it, NOTICE.md names what a built page carries of others' (three.js, Emscripten's runtime, musl, LLVM's libraries, dlmalloc), and every built page carries both at the top of its body. The standalone repository, runway-to-ring, has the same two files. LICENSING.md records the decision, the options that were open, and what was done.

    The kit says plainly that it is not legal advice.

    The local harness

    The work is meant to be done the way the owner built their Inspection Simulator: Claude Code on the Windows machine, driving Unreal Engine 5.8 through the engine's own MCP server (Experimental in 5.8: the ModelContextProtocol and AllToolsets plugins, on loopback, one editor and port per worker), Blender through Blender Lab's MCP server for the generators that make the Shuttle and other hero shapes (each also runnable from the command line), and Android Studio for the Quest build later. MCP is for looking, trying, importing, compiling and testing; anything that must exist next time is written as code and committed, so an MCP session is never the only record of how something was made. Long jobs (cooking, packaging) go through Unreal's automation tool from the shell, with the editor closed.

    The kit records the traps it found: Geometry Script mesh writes reported to crash packaged 5.8.3 builds (generate in the editor and bake), the Fab plugin's 0.0.22 update breaking C++ builds on launcher engines, no Lumen, Nanite or MegaLights on Quest, Epic and Meta naming different Android NDKs, and Cesium's ion data and Google's tiles not allowed offline. Each claim about Unreal is marked in SOURCES.md as read or only searched; the searched ones are unverified until opened on the owner's machine.

    The owner's open Ring

    The owner's idea for later (handoff/OPEN_RING_AIR.md): a Ring open to space, as in the film Elysium, its air held to the floor by the spin as the Earth's is held by the Earth, thick at the floor and gone at the hub. A craft landing on the deck would then go from vacuum to sea level in one approach, the aerodynamics changing all the way down, while everything turns. The note works the physics. In the turning frame a still, isothermal gas balances dp/dr = ρω²r, so

    At the sketch's 5 km an open Ring holds no air at all: the spin's pull is too weak over so short a height, so the air would fill the wheel at three quarters of the floor's pressure and pour out of the open sides. That is why the sketch's Ring has a glazed roof and sealed walls, and why its deck is a vacuum behind bulkheads: the sketch is consistent as it stands. Done faithfully, the idea wants a Bishop ring, Forrest Bishop's 1997 concept: about 1,000 km in radius with walls about 200 km high and an open top. There the air reaches near-vacuum about 120 km above the floor, and the deck landing becomes an entry: the air turns with the floor at up to 3.1 km/s, so a craft coming down from near the axis meets it at up to Mach 9, brakes, heats and glows, then glides to the deck.

    The owner's other way (21 November): keep the Ring at 5 km and make the air a rule of the world, one atmosphere at the floor and almost none 50 m up. That is a scale height of 11 m, which weight would need about 780 g to hold, so the note names it as a field, not gravity. The co-rotating approach then stays in vacuum, and the air comes in the last tens of metres, in the hover. It trades physical truth for the same Ring and a short, cheap, dramatic last 50 m; its one risk is that the rule must be the same for everything.

    Specified for the Unreal version (handoff/RING_AIR_SPEC.md, 21 November, on the owner's word). The band open to space all round, as in Elysium. The air one function: ordinary air the spin holds up to 30 m, sea level to a person and to anything built under 25 m; then a 20 m skin where a field holds it, touching nothing but the air; vacuum from 50 m. The autoland comes in antispinward on a 6° glide path fixed in the turning frame, a steady turn about the Ring's axis seen from the stars, its pull rising from 0.39 g at the fix to 1 g over the pad, then converts as the F-35B does, through the skin into the air, to a hover and a vertical landing: about 200 s and 1.3 t of propellant from the hold point, against the least-propellant crew's 692 kg, which the tour can spare. Sixteen acceptance tests, and the order to build it, core first. The Bishop ring is its appendix. Nothing of either is built.

    From the repository's root. The core and its tests, natively (CMake 3.25 or newer, any C++17 compiler):

    How a new part is added

    Contract first: the header and its tests before the code behind them. That is how docking, the walk, the flight home, the sky and the deck were built in parallel by separate sessions on one afternoon without stepping on each other, and how the flight director, the wind and the deck's planner came in after them.

    1. A header, core/orbit_<part>.h: its chosen constants, its C functions, its enums, and its readouts block (an enum of indices ending in OC_<PART>_COUNT, and oc_<part>_out() with oc_<part>_out_len()). Say in it which numbers are chosen and that none describes a real system.
    2. Its state in a struct of its own inside the core's State (as dock_state.h does), so a run replays, and folded into the hash, with a test that changes each field alone and sees the hash move.
    3. Its hooks in internal.h: reset, the step, the readouts, the hash, a crew mode if it has one; orbit_core.cpp calls them. A crew that is asked for and refuses changes nothing but its refusal's readout.
    4. Its exports, core/exports/<part>.txt, one function a line: what the page may call.
    5. Its tests, test/test_<part>.cpp, each named in a line reading // TEST: <name>, and the part added to the foreach in CMakeLists.txt. State physics: what is measured, against what, within what; and show each test able to fail.
    6. Its page module, web/src/<part>-view.js, listed in features.js, reading its block with core.addBlock(key, out, len, names), which refuses loudly if the page and the core disagree on its length; and a page test holding its names to the header.
    7. Its README section, with its numbers and the tests that show them, its keys in the table, and what is not in it; its sources in docs/provenance.md, section 1h.
