    1What it is

    A spaceplane takes off from KDFW runway 17C like an aeroplane, keeps climbing, reaches a 500 km orbit and closes on a ring station there. It docks at the station's hub. Its crew walks inside, where the floor curves up ahead and behind and meets itself overhead. It can land on the station's open flight deck as a jump jet lands on a ship. Then it flies home to 17C, through entry, and stops on the centreline.

    It is called Runway to Ring, and in the repository the orbit sketch. It was made in one day, 12 November 2026, on the owner's word and outside the roadmap of the repository it lives in, a32x-cbt, whose product is an A320 trainer. It stands apart from the A320: its own small flight core, its own page, and no file of the Airbus product changed.

    The one fiction

    The drive gives 706 kN, 1.2 times the take-off weight, at 2,000 s of specific impulse. That is the only thing in the sketch that physics does not allow today. At 450 s, the best of chemical rockets, half the craft's mass in propellant would buy about 3 km/s, a third of what orbit takes. At 2,000 s the crew reaches orbit having spent 10.92 km/s of rocket-equation velocity and keeps 4.39 t of propellant, about 2.7 km/s more. Everything else is ordinary mechanics: gravity, the 1976 standard atmosphere, the lift of a slender delta, a spinning wheel.

    The loop, flown by the crew

    The page lets you take the controls at any point and give them back: the crew picks up from wherever the craft is. The walk inside the Ring and the deck landing are side trips from the hub. The Sun, the Moon and the stars stand where they really were over KDFW at 19:00 UTC on 12 November 2026, one in the afternoon in Texas, or on any date from 1900 to 2100.

    How the stick flies

    In the air the stick moves pitch and bank targets that the flight law holds, turning in balance, much as a fly-by-wire airliner's law does: let go and the craft keeps the attitude it was given. Above the air, where the dynamic pressure is under 400 Pa (the law hands back below 200 Pa), the stick commands rates and the craft holds the attitude it is left at, or points where 1 to 4 say. The readouts' AIR LAW or RATE line says which law is flying.

    On a touch screen a stick sits under the left thumb and a throttle under the right; between them the six buttons used most (TOUR, CREW, TO ORBIT, VIEW, TIME +, MORE), and MORE opens the rest (figure ). A gamepad with the standard mapping works as the table says, and its buttons fire on the press.

    3The loop, part by part

    Each part below says what it does, how to fly it, and the numbers its tests hold it to. The core is in parts too (section 4), and each part's test program is named in the tables.

    3.1The take-off and the climb to orbit

    The crew holds the brakes for 20 s, then flies the whole ascent: 12½ minutes to orbit. It takes off due south, as 17C points, rotates at 95 m/s, and from 300 m turns left, banked 30°, to the launch azimuth, 090. A pitch schedule carries it through the air, the throttle eased through the region of highest dynamic pressure. Above the air it steers by a cut-down version of the Space Shuttle's powered explicit guidance: the sine of the thrust's elevation is linear in time, and the line is solved again each step so that the burn ends at the Ring's height with no climb left and a round orbit's speed.

    What it flies on

    • The Earth: a sphere of 6,371 km, its gravity a point mass's, turning once a sidereal day. The ground is level to the local plumb line, gravity and the Earth's turn together, so a parked craft stays parked (it moves under 1 cm in 30 s, ground_settles).
    • The air: the 1976 standard atmosphere to 86 km, held within 0.5 % of its tables to 80 km; above it, exponentials through the standard's densities at 86, 120, 200 and 500 km, within 20 %. No wind.
    • The craft: a delta lifting body of 150 m² and 18 m span, its lift Polhamus's for a slender delta blending to Newtonian impact from Mach 1.5 to 5, with static and damping derivatives and three gear legs. A rate loop by dynamic inversion flies it inside what the surfaces (by dynamic pressure), the small thrusters and the drive's gimbal can give.
    • The step: 0.02 s, fifty a second, by fourth-order Runge–Kutta in an inertial frame. Time can run 100 times faster; past a cap the page drops time rather than stretch a step.

    Flying it yourself

    Take the controls on the runway or anywhere in the climb. On the runway Q E steer the nose wheel; full throttle (Z) and the brakes off (B) start the roll, and a pull at about 95 m/s lifts the nose. In the air the stick sets pitch and bank and the law holds them. A take-off by hand at full power is airborne within 30 s; half the stick to the right asks 22.5° of bank, and let go, the law holds 18 to 27° with under 1° of sideslip (hand_flown_takeoff). Give the controls back at any height and the crew takes the climb from where it is: from a hand-flown climb at 3 km it still reaches orbit, 500.6 × 499.8 km at 727 s (crew_takes_over).

    Above the air there is nothing to bank against: the stick turns the craft at a rate, and 1 holds it where it is left, 2 and 3 point it along or against its motion through space, 4 at the Ring. Burning prograde raises the far side of the orbit; the map (section 3.9) shows the orbit's new shape as it changes.

    Why 32.84°? KDFW is at 32.9° north. A launch due east from there gives an orbit inclined at about the site's latitude, the least inclination reachable without a turn in space; the orbit's plane then passes over KDFW once a day, which is what the flight home waits for.

    3.2Orbit and the Ring

    The Ring waits in a round 500 km orbit in the crew's plane, 25 km ahead of where the crew arrives: at reset the core flies the crew once, unseen, to find that place. A real launch window is the inverse problem, the launch timed to the station; this sketch does not solve it. The Ring's spin axis is fixed among the stars, as a spinning wheel's is, set along the track where the crew arrives, so the hub's port faces the arriving craft.

    The approach (T, APPROACH) takes the craft from where it reaches orbit to the hold point, 300 m off the hub's port on its axis, in 730 s on the small thrusters alone, the drive's propellant untouched (approach_holds). It goes round the outside of the Ring when it must. It is offered only where it makes sense: in orbit, within 50 km of the Ring and passing it under 50 m/s in the frame turning with its orbit. An audit found that the approach engaged from 380 km behind flew the craft into the ground; it is gated now, and from 48 km it reaches the hold point in 1,544 s with the lowest point of the orbit never under 487 km (approach_gated_like_docking).

    3.3The approach and docking

    The craft's port is a probe on its nose, on the docking axis, its head 18 m ahead of the centre of mass and 2 m past the nose: the pilot looks straight down the axis from the cockpit, and the laws that point the nose point the port. The hub's port is a drogue, a cone with a socket, at the centre of the hub's face on the Ring's axis, painted round with a target 36 m across: a bullseye, an alignment cross, and the key, a broad orange bar. The face turns with the Ring, 2.54° a second, once in 142 s. To dock is to line up on the axis, roll into phase with the key and turn with it, right wing down at the Ring's spin, and close slowly.

    3.4The walk inside the Ring

    Off the craft, through the hatch into the hub, down a spoke in a lift, and out onto the floor. Enter or WALK starts it when the craft is docked at the hub; at the hatch, E goes back aboard and the flight returns. The walk has its own core, core/walk.cpp, on the flight's fixed steps, and its own page, web/src/walk-view.js, which draws the whole frame while walking. The flight goes on underneath: the Ring keeps turning and orbiting.

    The physics is the Ring's turning frame and nothing else. A body in it feels the centrifugal pull ω²r outward and Coriolis's −2ω × v, and whatever holds it. Seen from outside, a body let go goes straight; the tests work each motion out again that way and hold the two equal. Up is toward the axis everywhere, so a jumper's turn about the axis speeds up on the way up, and a jump lands spinward, where on the Earth it lands to the west, against the spin.

    The lift's ride is chosen for its riders. Coriolis's push, 2ωv, is sideways and the same at any height, so the cruise is the speed that holds it to 1.5 m/s², 16.9 m/s. At the hub the riders weigh three hundredths of a g, and a car that left faster than they fall would leave them on its ceiling: it leaves, and arrives, at half their weight. At the floor it starts and stops at 1 m/s². While it moves the riders hold its rail.

    The page draws three passes: the real sky first (the flight's own Earth, air and stars, from where the eye is as the Ring turns), then the Ring's inside over it, far and near. The Sun lights the band only where it gets in over a rim wall's top, worked out for each pixel. On 12 November it stands 23.7° out of the Ring's plane, so the middle of the band has about 50 s of sunlight in each turn of 142 s, and the strip under the wall on the Sun's side none. The roof's lamps and the Earth's light fill the rest.

    3.5The flight deck

    The Ring has a deck: an open sector of its band, 1,500 m of the floor's arc and the band's whole 1 km across, midway between spokes 0 and 1. The roof stops at the sector's ends; at each end a pressure bulkhead 20 m thick stands from the floor to the roof. Three pads, 25 m in radius, lie on the centreline 400 m apart. It was the owner's idea: come in from the rim, match the Ring's turn and curve, fly an airliner's approach down a curved deck, then hover to a landing on a pad, as an F-35B does.

    Why it is a hover

    In the Ring's own frame, which turns once in 142 s, the floor stands still and everything over it is pulled outward at ω²r, one g at the floor: a craft holding still over a spot of the deck must thrust toward the axis at that. Seen from the stars it is moving with the floor at ωr, 221.5 m/s, and the thrust bends its path round. A craft at rest among the stars feels no pull at all, and the deck sweeps past under it. Moving at u against the deck, along it, the pull is (ωr + u)²/r: flying spinward makes the craft heavier, antispinward lighter. So to land is to take up the deck's speed while coming down onto it, and the weight grows from nothing to one g as the speed against the deck goes.

    The craft as a jet

    The drive swivels, as an F-35B's does, from aft to straight down through the centre of mass, at 15° a second: the same drive, the same flow of propellant. Jet-borne, roll posts in the wings and a lift jet in the nose bleed up to 5 % of its thrust for attitude. Each law asks for the accelerations it wants along the deck, above it and across it, and gets the thrust from the exact equations of motion in a cylinder about the Ring's axis: the turning frame's pulls are worked out, not left to feedback, and the tides are fed forward.

    The crew's landing

    From the hold point (or anywhere near the Ring, by docking's way round it) the crew flies out to a point 400 m over the floor, beside the spokes' plane, at rest among the stars: the deck sweeps by underneath at 204 m/s, and the craft waits, weightless, until the nearest pad is far enough ahead. Then the glide, the airliner's part: along the deck at 400 m, the speed against it bleeding away on a schedule by the distance to go, the swivel turning down as the weight comes on. Past the last spoke the course leaves its side for the centreline and steepens down through the roof's gap. Then the jet's part: a hover 20 m over the pad's centre, and a descent at 2 m/s easing to 0.25 m/s at the touch. The craft comes in tail first: the deck overtakes it, and the nose points the way the craft must be pushed.

    By hand

    6 is the deck hold, the F-35B's unified control in the deck's frame. The stick is a velocity against the deck, 10 m/s at its stops; centred, the hold keeps the craft's place. The throttle is the climb or the sink, 8 m/s at its stops; its middle holds the height, and near the deck the sink eases to a touchdown's, so a shut throttle lands the craft. Q E turn the nose. Taking the controls from the crew over the deck gives this hold, never the plain one, which would let the craft fall.

    A first landing by hand

    1. From the hold point (T, or docked and let go), press C: the crew flies out, waits for the pad and glides in.
    2. When the status line reads HOVER, press Space. You have the deck hold, the throttle at its middle: the craft stays where it is, 20 m up.
    3. Fly it over the pad's centre with W A S D and let go; the hold keeps the place. The approach display's PLAN shows the pad.
    4. Shut the throttle (X): the sink eases to a touchdown's near the deck and the craft lands. On the deck, Shift lifts off again.

    3.6The flight home

    RETURN hands the craft to the crew to fly home to KDFW 17C from wherever it is in orbit: the insertion orbit, held off the hub (it backs away first, on the small thrusters), docked (it lets go first), or an orbit the pilot has changed. Nothing in it writes where the craft is: it commands body rates, the throttle, the gear and the brakes, as the crew to orbit does, and if its guidance cannot solve the flight, the craft does not arrive.

    • The plan. The orbit's plane stands still among the stars while the Earth turns KDFW under it. KDFW lies at almost the orbit's inclination, so it passes close to the plane once a day. Within 1,200 km of the plane, the plan's limit, the runway is in reach for about 3½ hours either side of that passing: four or five passes a day, and a wait of up to about 19 hours. RETURN again, or TO ORBIT, skips to a minute before the burn.
    • The burn. Tail first at 30 % thrust until the orbit's low point is 60 km: 121 to 127 m/s from the round orbit.
    • The entry. The nose 40° above the air's flow, the bank's size setting the range and its side the cross-range, reversing when the runway's bearing drifts past a deadband. A predictor flies the rest of the entry with the core's air and lift every 2 s, and a corrector moves the bank until it ends at the gate, 200 km of path short of the threshold at 800 m/s.
    • What it learns. Each step it feels its own acceleration and compares the drag and lift in it with its model's; fading-memory filters keep the ratios, and the predictor and the approach fly by them.
    • The terminal area and the approach. From Mach 2.6 and about 31 km, a glide to a circle of 5 km radius that touches the extended centreline 14 km north of the threshold, round it onto a long final; the drive relit at low throttle, the 3° glide path flown as an airliner flies it, gear down, a flare from 12 m, the brakes, the nose wheel on the line.

    3.7The Sun, the Moon and the stars

    The flight has a date: 12 November 2026 at 19:00:00 UTC unless the address says otherwise. That is one in the afternoon at KDFW, the Sun 38° up in the south-south-west and still 32° up where the crew reaches the Ring; a thin Moon, 11 % lit, stands 23° up in the south-south-east. From there the sky moves with the flight's clock: the Moon its half a degree an hour among the stars, the Sun its degree a day. Nothing in the flight depends on the date.

    The core works the sky out by J. The eye is not at the Earth's centre: from the runway the Moon stands 0.83° lower than from there, and the page draws it from wherever the eye is.

    • The Moon is a sphere of its true angular size along the true line to it, turned as the core says, lit by the Sun at its phase, with earthshine against a dark sky.     3.9The sound

    Every sound is made as it plays, by the browser's own Web Audio nodes: no sound file, sample or recording anywhere. It reads the readouts and writes nothing back. The ear is aboard in the cockpit view and with the camera in the others, and sound reaches it three ways: through the air (the drive's roar and crackle, the wind, an entry's plasma, each as strong as the air is dense, so the drive fades with height to nothing), through the structure (the drive's rumble and eight hull modes that ring under load, all there is in a vacuum), and in the cabin (fans and a 400 Hz hum). A camera outside hears the drive late by the time sound takes, thinned with distance; past the speed of sound only a camera inside the drive's Mach cone hears it.

    4How it is built

    Two halves meet at one seam. The core is C++ and knows nothing of drawing; the page is JavaScript and owns nothing that moves. The page steps the core, reads its readouts, and draws them.

    The core

    flight/orbit/core is C++17 that includes only <cmath>, <cstdint> and <cstring>: no engine, no clock, no random source, no thread, no file. It advances only in whole fixed steps of 0.02 s, fifty a second, by fourth-order Runge–Kutta in an Earth-centred inertial frame; the readouts are given in the site's east, north and up, turning with the Earth. Time is the step count. The same inputs give the same state, bit for bit, and a hash of the whole state (oc_hash) shows it. The crew, the approach, docking, the deck and the flight home live inside it as modes that command only rates, throttle, thrusters, gear and brakes, as a pilot does.

    It is one program in parts, each with its own contract: a public header with its constants, its C functions and a readouts block (an array of doubles the page reads after every step, named by an enum); its exports, one function a line, in core/exports/<part>.txt; its own state inside the core's State, so a run replays; and its own test program, test/test_<part>.cpp, whose tests CMake registers from lines reading // TEST: <name>. What the parts share is core/internal.h. The Ring's shape is one set of numbers in orbit_core.h that the core, the drawing and the tests all use.

    The same flight, every time. flight/orbit/build/orbit_run 10 800 flies the crew to 800 s and prints the flight every 10 s and a hash of the state. At 599e051 the hash is ea7847fe natively, under g++ 13.3 and clang 18 alike, from -O0 to -O3, and 370241c5 as WebAssembly: each binary repeats itself bit for bit, and the two builds agree to every digit printed but not to the bit, since their C libraries' sin and exp differ in the last place.

    The page

    flight/orbit/web is plain JavaScript modules and three.js 0.186, bundled with esbuild (both from flight/web's packages) into one HTML file that opens from file:// and fetches nothing: about 1.9 MB, of which the core as WebAssembly is 0.3 MB and the same core compiled to plain JavaScript another 0.3 MB. Where a host will not run WebAssembly the page runs the plain JavaScript and says so in its status line. Every texture is painted by code; no image, model, font or sound file ships. The coastlines, the towns, the Moon's features and the stars are data files made by tools in web/tools/ from public-domain or CC0 sources, each tool saying where its inputs come from.

    main.js keeps the core stepping at its fixed rate: each frame it adds the frame's time, times the warp, to a debt, runs whole steps, and drops the debt past a cap rather than stretch a step. The camera sits at the origin of what is drawn and everything is placed from it, in two passes: the far one (the Earth, its air, the stars, anything past 300 km) and the near one, so a centimetre at the wheels and the Earth's whole disc both have depth. The Earth is drawn per pixel, each ray meeting the sphere exactly, so it is as round from the runway as from orbit, under a single-scattering atmosphere.

    Each part beyond the flight is a module of its own listed in features.js, with the same few hooks: init(ctx) once; command(name, ctx) for a key or a button, true if it took it; update(ctx) each frame before drawing; hud(ctx) for more readout lines; status(ctx) for the status line; and owns(ctx) with frame(ctx, dt) for a part that draws the whole frame itself, as the walk does. A part reads the core's readouts and gives commands; none writes the flight's state. The page keeps a handle, window.orbitSketch, for tests and for tools such as this guide's: the core, the page's state, and command(name).

    The audit

    At about 15:00 UTC an independent, read-only audit of the physics was run against commit c56d77b: the dynamics, the aerodynamics, the laws, the gear, the orbit maths, the guidance, the Ring, docking, the walk, the frames, the atmosphere, the Earth's shader, the page and the sound. Its verified defects were fixed, each with a test that fails without the fix (commit 62c5ff0):

    • the crew's approach from far out flew into the ground; the approach is now gated as docking is;
    • Polhamus's lift pointed into the windward face past 155° of angle of attack, in a tail slide (230 angles wrong before, none after);
    • the spokes ended 80 m short of the roof in the contact model, so a craft in the spokes' plane above the roof was never hit;
    • the insertion guidance kept a burn time updated after the solve, so its plan missed its own cutoff by up to 15 m/s and 750 m;
    • a camera outside, in vacuum, still heard the hull; and the readouts' AIR LAW guessed the law where the core now says it.

    Commit 70d5682 then fixed three tests the audit found passing for the wrong reason: an attitude hold that let the attitude drift 23°, and two docking verdicts (sliding, angled) that no test reached. The audit's suspicions it could not settle are listed as G7 to G11 in section 5. An earlier rigour pass had found three defects of its own (the hash missed fields, a control that is not a number poisoned the state, an escape path's lowest point read wrong), fixed at the merge a3556c9.

    5Where it left off

    Nothing is furnished and nothing is finished: the README says so in its first paragraph. This is where each part stood on 12 November 2026.

    What is solid

    • The loop flies itself end to end. Take-off to orbit, the approach, docking, the walk, the deck landing and the flight home each run under the crew from where the last one left the craft, and the whole loop runs in one test (lands_after_undocking). The tour flies it on the page.
    • The core is deterministic and watched. One hash of the whole state, replay in pieces, conservation and work-energy ledgers with derived tolerances, no NaN under abuse, every readout re-derived, every constant certified.
    • The physics is stated, not tuned. The walk's Coriolis and the deck's hover are worked out against the view from outside the Ring; the Moon is held to Horizons; the flight home survives a wrong model 200 times in 200 within the narrower pool.
    • The page runs anywhere a browser does, from a phone to a desktop card, from one file, with no network.

    The sketch is a proof that the airframe, the drive and the Ring work together, flown in a browser. The game it points at, with the working title Meridian Ring, is meant for Unreal Engine 5.8. Nothing of the Unreal version exists yet. What exists is a kit to start it from cold.

    The decision to make first: the licence

    The sketch is GPLv3, because everything in flight/ is, by this repository's rule. Unreal Engine's EULA forbids combining the engine with GPL code, and the GPL wants the whole of a combined work under the GPL, which Epic's code cannot be. So the core, as licensed today, cannot go into a shipped Unreal game. But it is the owner's to relicense: it links no third-party GPL code (only <cmath>, <cstdint>, <cstring>), takes nothing from A32NX or JSBSim, and what it took from outside is method or public domain. LICENSING.md sets out three options and recommends the first:

    1. Relicense the sketch's own code (MIT or Apache-2.0) before it goes into the Unreal project; the copy here can stay GPLv3 or be dual-licensed, written down in both repositories.
    2. Keep it GPLv3 and out of the game's binary: the core as its own program, Unreal talking to it over a local socket. A grey zone; take advice before shipping on it.
    3. Write a new core from the canon: possible, and wasteful.

    The kit says plainly that it is not legal advice. Nothing has been relicensed.

    The local harness

    The work is meant to be done the way the owner built his Inspection Simulator: Claude Code on the Windows machine, driving Unreal Engine 5.8 through the engine's own MCP server (Experimental in 5.8: the ModelContextProtocol and AllToolsets plugins, on loopback, one editor and port per worker), Blender through Blender Lab's MCP server for the generators that make the Shuttle and other hero shapes (each also runnable from the command line), and Android Studio for the Quest build later.     The owner's open Ring

    The owner's idea for later (handoff/OPEN_RING_AIR.md): a Ring open to space, as in the film Elysium, its air held to the floor by the spin as the Earth's is held by the Earth, thick at the floor and gone at the hub. A craft landing on the deck would then go from vacuum to sea level in one approach, the aerodynamics changing all the way down, while everything turns. The note works the physics. In the turning frame a still, isothermal gas balances dp/dr = ρω²r, so

    p(r) = p_floor · exp( −(R² − r²) / (2 R H) ),   ω²R = g,   H = R_s T / g = 8,434 m at 288 K
    radiusspin periodfloor speedair at the axis, of the floor'sthins to a millionth at
    5 km, this sketch142 s221 m/s74 %never
    50 km449 s700 m/s5.2 %never

    At the sketch's 5 km an open Ring holds no air at all: the spin's pull is too weak over so short a height, so the air would fill the wheel at three quarters of the floor's pressure and pour out of the open sides. That is why the sketch's Ring has a glazed roof and sealed walls, and why its deck is a vacuum behind bulkheads: the sketch is consistent as it stands. Done faithfully, the idea wants a Bishop ring, Forrest Bishop's 1997 concept: about 1,000 km in radius with walls about 200 km high and an open top. There the air reaches near-vacuum about 120 km above the floor, and the deck landing becomes an entry: the air turns with the floor at up to 3.1 km/s, so a craft coming down from near the axis, at rest among the stars, meets it at up to Mach 9, brakes, heats and glows, then glides to the deck. The air does most of the rotation-matching, as the Earth's air takes 7.6 km/s off a craft coming home. The model would need the air turning with the Ring, density by the formula above, gravity falling in a straight line toward the axis, entry heating as return.cpp already has it, and the deck's guidance starting from the bottom of an entry; the structure would need streaming from orbit down to a pad. Nothing of it is built.

    How a new part is added

    Contract first: the header and its tests before the code behind them. That is how docking, the walk, the flight home, the sky and the deck were built in parallel by separate sessions on one afternoon without stepping on each other.

    1. A header, core/orbit_<part>.h: its chosen constants, its C functions, its enums, and its readouts block (an enum of indices ending in OC_<PART>_COUNT, and oc_<part>_out() with oc_<part>_out_len()). Say in it which numbers are chosen and that none describes a real system.
    2. Its state in a struct of its own inside the core's State (as dock_state.h does), so a run replays, and folded into the hash.
    3. Its hooks in internal.h: reset, the step, the readouts, the hash, a crew mode if it has one; orbit_core.cpp calls them.
    4. Its exports, core/exports/<part>.txt, one function a line: what the page may call.
    5. Its tests, test/test_<part>.cpp, each named in a line reading // TEST: <name>, and the part added to the foreach in CMakeLists.txt. State physics: what is measured, against what, within what.
    6. Its page module, web/src/<part>-view.js, listed in features.js, reading its block with core.addBlock(key, out, len, names), which refuses loudly if the page and the core disagree on its length; and a page test holding its names to the header.
    7. Its README section, with its numbers and the tests that show them, its keys in the table, and what is not in it; its sources in docs/provenance.md, section 1h.
